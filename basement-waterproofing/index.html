Find the water source before you spend anything

Spending money on a drain system for a leaking supply pipe wastes it. Spending money on a pipe repair for groundwater seepage does the same. The order of work matters more than the product you pick.

Four common sources of basement moisture
SourceHow it showsSimple test
Plumbing leakDamp spot near a pipe, drain or water heater, grows without rainTurn off all water, read the meter, see if it still moves
Groundwater seepageWater at the floor and wall joint, worse after wet weatherTape foil to the wall, check which side collects water
CondensationCold pipes sweat, walls feel damp in humid monthsFoil droplets on the room side point here
Surface runoffWater at window wells or one wall after rain or wateringWatch gutters, downspouts and soil slope during a storm

If the meter moves with everything off, the leak is in a pipe. Leak detection covers how we locate it without tearing up the floor.

Local ground

What Riverton ground can do to a basement

The Utah Geological Survey's Special Study 152 covers the Copperton quadrangle, which includes parts of Riverton. It maps shallow groundwater, which the study says can flood basements and other underground facilities. It also maps collapsible soils, which lose volume when wet, and expansive soils, which swell when wet and shrink when dry.

Collapsible soil can settle and strain buried pipes. Expansive soil can crack foundations and damage underground utilities. The study does not say which Riverton blocks sit on which type, so treat this as a reason to check your home, not as a verdict.

The practical takeaway: movement and moisture often go together. If you see new cracks or a drain line that has started leaking, tell us. A water problem and a settling problem can be linked.

Interior drains, sump pits and discharge

When groundwater is the cause, plumbers usually manage it from the inside. A drain channel runs along the base of the wall and carries water to a sump pit. A pump then lifts the water out through a discharge pipe.

1

Confirm the source

Meter test, moisture checks and a look at gutters, grading and window wells.

2

Open the slab edge

A channel is cut along the problem wall or walls, then trenched and graded.

3

Set the drain and pit

Drain pipe goes into the channel and slopes toward the pit, which gets a liner and a lid.

4

Install the pump

A pump, float and check valve are set, with backup or alarm if chosen.

5

Route the discharge

Water leaves the house through a pipe that terminates away from the foundation.

Discharge rules vary, so ask the Riverton building department before choosing a route. The city says a building permit is required for work on plumbing systems the code regulates. Our sump pump page covers the pump itself.

Window wells, downspouts and plumbing mistaken for seepage

Window wells fill with rain and irrigation runoff, and a well without a working drain acts like a bucket against your wall. Downspouts that dump at the foundation do the same. Fixing either may solve a damp wall without any digging inside.

Riverton has a secondary irrigation system, which the city turns on tentatively around May 1. A sprinkler line break or a head that sprays toward the house can soak one wall for weeks. A broken sprinkler line is often stoppable at the property's ball valve.

Plumbing leaks are the most commonly overlooked cause. A pinhole in a copper line or a failing drain joint can mimic seepage for months. If the dampness is near a bathroom, laundry or water heater, check there first.
