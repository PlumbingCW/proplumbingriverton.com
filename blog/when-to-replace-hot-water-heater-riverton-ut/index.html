When to Replace a Hot Water Heater in Riverton, UT: A Symptom Checklist

Replace the heater when the tank itself is leaking, because a leaking tank cannot be patched. Repair it when the fault is in a part, such as a thermostat, a heating element, a valve or a fitting.

Most decisions fall between those two. Use the checklist below to sort what you are seeing, then match it to the cheapest safe fix.

By the Riverton Pro Plumbing team · Published October 29, 2026 · Updated October 29, 2026

Start here

Find where the water is coming from

Before anything else, work out whether the water on the floor is coming from the tank or from something attached to it. Dry the base with a towel, then watch it with a flashlight. Water running out from under the jacket, or beading on the bottom of the tank, points to the tank. Drips from the pipe on top, the valve on the side, or the drain at the bottom point to a part.

A tank leak means the steel shell has corroded through. It does not stop and it is not repairable. If you see one, shut off the cold supply to the heater, turn the gas control to off or switch off the breaker, and call us. Gas smell is a different case: leave the building and call Enbridge Gas Utah at 1-800-767-1689 or 911 first.

Age, honestly

Where age fits in

Manufacturers and plumbers commonly say that many tank heaters are in their last years around a decade, and that the later years are when failures bunch up. Treat that as a rough reminder, not a rule. A well-maintained tank can run longer, and a neglected one can quit sooner.

The data plate on the tank carries the serial number, and the maker's manual or support line can decode the build date from it. If that is unclear, call us and we will help you read it. An older heater with a spotless base and steady hot water is in a different position from the same heater with rust at the drain valve.

If a heater is old and has just failed in a way that needs a repair costing a fair share of a new unit, replacement usually makes better sense. If it is old and merely a little slow to heat, ask for a diagnosis before you spend on a new one.

What Riverton homeowners should know

Utah's statewide plumbing amendments require water heaters to be anchored or strapped against horizontal movement from earthquake motion, with straps in the upper one-third and lower one-third of the heater's height. If your old heater was never strapped, a replacement is the time to fix it. Check any heater you plan to keep, too.

Riverton says a building permit is required for work on plumbing systems regulated by the code, and applications go by email to the city's building inspections office. The city page does not say whether a like-for-like heater swap needs one, so ask the Riverton building department before work starts. Utah Code 15A-2-103 adopts the 2024 International Plumbing Code effective 7/1/2026, and your plumber should be working to the current code.

Sources: Utah Code 15A-3-305, Utah Code 15A-2-103, Riverton Building Inspections and Permits page

Parts that fail

Six problems that look scary but are often repairable

No heat on an electric unit

A burned-out upper or lower element, or a thermostat that has stopped switching, is a common reason. Both are replaceable parts.

Pilot will not stay lit

A weak thermocouple or a draft at the burner can be the cause. A plumber tests it before you decide the heater is finished.

Relief valve drips

A weeping valve may simply be worn. It may also be reacting to a pressure or expansion problem in the house, which the plumber should check.

Fitting leaks on top

A loose or corroded connector at the inlet or outlet can be reconnected or replaced. See our water heater repair work for how we sort these.

Drain valve drips

A cheap valve can be replaced, though it should be done with care because old drain valves can break when disturbed.

Thermostat set wrong

Sometimes the answer is a dial. A plumber can check for a scalding or lukewarm setting before any part is swapped.

Signs to act soon

When waiting costs more than fixing

  • ✓Water on the floor that returns after you dry it.If the tank is the source, the leak will grow. Water damage to flooring and drywall costs more than the heater.
  • ✓A metallic smell or rust in hot water only.Cold water that runs clear while hot water rusts suggests the inside of the tank, not the pipes.
  • ✓Rumbling that has gotten louder.Sediment hardens on the bottom over time. A flush may help when caught early, and a heavily scaled tank may be past saving.
  • ✓A scorched or sooty look around the burner or flue.This is a safety issue on gas heaters. Shut the heater off and call a plumber.

Planning the swap

What a replacement usually involves

1

Shut off and drain the old tank

The plumber closes the water supply, turns off the gas or power, and drains the tank to a safe spot.

2

Disconnect and remove it

Water lines, gas connector or electrical feed, vent and relief discharge line come off. The old heater is hauled away.

3

Set the new heater and its pan

The new tank goes in with a drain pan if the location calls for one, and the relief valve and discharge line get connected.

4

Strap it

Utah requires water heaters to be anchored or strapped against earthquake motion. A plumber installs the straps.

5

Fill, vent, test

The tank is filled first, then the gas or power comes on. Fittings are checked for leaks and the burner or elements are tested.

Timing depends on the location, the vent and any code upgrades the old install needs. We do not promise a clock time. Hot water returns once the new tank has heated.

Water heater replacement questions

Dry the base and watch where new water appears. Water from under the tank jacket or the bottom edge usually means the shell has failed. Water from a pipe, valve or fitting is a part problem. When in doubt, shut the water to the heater and have it checked.

If the heater is old, has rust in the water, or has started rumbling loudly, planning ahead is cheaper than an emergency replacement. A failed tank can flood a utility room. A planned swap lets you choose the unit instead of taking what is available.

If the repair is a thermostat, element, valve or connector and the tank is dry, it often is. If the tank is leaking or rusting inside, replacement is nearly always the sound choice. A plumber can tell you after looking at it.

On electric units a failed lower element is a common cause. On any tank, sediment can reduce the usable volume. A plumber tests the elements or burner, then flushes the tank if sediment is the issue.
