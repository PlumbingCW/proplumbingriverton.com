How to Repair a Leaking Toilet Tank in Riverton, UT

A leaking toilet tank almost always comes from one of four places: the supply line and shutoff valve, the tank-to-bowl bolts and gaskets, the fill valve, or a hairline crack in the porcelain. A few minutes with a dry paper towel and a little food coloring will tell you which one.

This guide walks through that sorting process, then covers the parts you can safely swap yourself and the point where toilet repair is better left to a licensed plumber.

Triage

First, work out where the water is really coming from

Do not start by buying parts. Water on the bathroom floor can come from the supply line, the tank, the base of the bowl, or just condensation, and each one has a different fix.

Dry the toilet completely with a towel, including the supply line, the shutoff valve and the underside of the tank. Wrap dry toilet paper around each joint and wait. The first piece of paper that darkens points to the source.

Test

The dye test that finds a silent leak

A leak you can hear but not see is usually inside the toilet, where water slips from the tank into the bowl past a worn flapper. It wastes water without leaving a puddle, and it is the most common reason a toilet keeps refilling by itself.

1

Add color to the tank

Lift the lid and put several drops of food coloring into the tank water. Do not flush.

2

Wait and watch the bowl

Leave the toilet alone for a good while. If color shows up in the bowl, water is leaking past the flapper or flush valve seat.

3

Rub the flapper edge

Turn off the water at the shutoff valve, flush to empty the tank, and run a finger around the flapper's sealing edge. Slime, grit or mineral crust there means the seal is not closing cleanly.

4

Check the chain

A chain too tight holds the flapper slightly open. One too loose can get caught under it. Aim for a little slack so the flapper drops fully.

5

Replace the flapper if the edge is worn

A new flapper is a small, inexpensive part that clips on the overflow tube posts. Match it to your flush valve opening before you buy.

Parts

Which parts fail and how to deal with each

The flapper and the flush valve seat

The flapper is a rubber stopper that lifts when you flush and drops back to seal the tank. Rubber hardens and warps over time, and mineral buildup can keep it from sealing flat. If cleaning the seat with a non-scratch pad does not stop the dye from leaking through, replace the flapper.

The fill valve

The fill valve refills the tank after each flush. When it fails, water keeps running into the overflow tube and the tank never fully shuts off. You will hear a hiss or a trickle. Adjusting the float or replacing the whole fill valve assembly usually fixes it, and it is one of the easier jobs to do yourself.

Tank bolts and the tank-to-bowl gasket

Two long bolts with rubber washers clamp the tank to the bowl. If water drips from under the tank, the washers have usually hardened or the gasket has flattened. Turn off the water, empty the tank, and replace the bolts, washers and gasket together. Tighten them evenly and gently, because overtightening cracks porcelain.

The supply line and shutoff valve

A braided supply line that is kinked, corroded at the nut, or simply old can weep at the connection. Replacing it is a hand-tool job. If the shutoff valve itself leaks around its stem or will not close, the repair becomes a valve repair job, and a stuck valve you force can break. That is the time to call.

Stop the water

What to do right now if the toilet is overflowing or the tank is pouring

Reach behind the toilet and turn the shutoff valve clockwise until it stops. That cuts the supply and ends most active leaks immediately. If the valve spins freely or will not turn, shut the main water valve to the house instead.

Lift the tank lid and push the float or flapper down if the water is rising toward the rim. Then mop up. Standing water on a bathroom floor seeps under flooring and can ruin the subfloor if left for days.

Know your limits

DIY fix or a plumber? A plain-language rule

Reasonable to do yourself

  • Replacing a flapper, fill valve or flush handle
  • Swapping a braided supply line and tightening the nut
  • Replacing tank bolts and washers with the water off
  • Adjusting the chain or float height

Call a licensed plumber

  • Water at the base of the bowl, which means a wax ring or flange problem
  • A shutoff valve that leaks, sticks or will not turn
  • Cracked porcelain on the tank or the bowl
  • A toilet that rocks, a floor that feels soft, or a leak that returns after repairs

The wax ring is the big dividing line. Pulling a toilet to reset it means disconnecting the supply, lifting a heavy fixture, and resealing a connection to the drain. A bad flange or a damaged floor under it turns a short job into a repair that needs the right parts and a steady hand.

Prevention

Keeping the next leak from starting

Most tank leaks are wear, and wear is slow. Once a year, look inside the tank, press the flapper edge with a finger, and check the supply line for stains. Replacing a soft flapper before it fails costs less than the water it would waste.

If you are also seeing a dripping sink or a stubborn faucet in the same bathroom, a visit that covers all of them saves a second trip. We also dispatch to Herriman and South Jordan, and the advice is the same there: older rubber parts tend to wear out around the same time.
